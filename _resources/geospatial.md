# Introduction to Geospatial Science

Geospatial technology encompasses a wide range of tools, methods, and disciplines used to understand **where things are, how they are distributed across space, and how they change over time**.

Geospatial approaches are used in nearly every field, from environmental science and public health to business, transportation, agriculture, emergency management, and urban planning. Whether you are interested in analyzing maps, studying environmental change, working with data, or building applications, geospatial skills can be useful across a wide range of careers.

---

## What is Geospatial Science?

**Geospatial science** is the study of information that has a geographic or spatial component. In other words, it focuses on understanding **where things are, how they relate to one another, and how those relationships change over time**.

For example, a researcher might use geospatial data to ask:

- Where are different types of vegetation located?
- How far is one location from another?
- Which communities are most vulnerable to flooding?
- How has a glacier changed over the past 50 years?
- Where do animal species migrate throughout the year?
- How has a city expanded over time?
- Where are public services most accessible?
- How do environmental conditions vary across a landscape?

Geospatial science provides tools and techniques for answering these kinds of questions using **maps, geographic data, satellite imagery, GPS, remote sensing, spatial analysis, and computer-based mapping technologies**.

---

## What is Geospatial Data?

**Geospatial data** is information that includes a location or geographic reference.

For example, a traditional dataset might contain information about a group of trees:

| Species | Height | Age |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Ponderosa Pine | 18 m | 42 years |
| Aspen | 12 m | 35 years |
| Douglas Fir | 25 m | 67 years |

A geospatial dataset could include the same information along with the **location of each tree**:

| Species | Height | Age | Latitude | Longitude |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Ponderosa Pine | 18 m | 42 years | 40.58 | -105.08 |
| Aspen | 12 m | 35 years | 40.59 | -105.07 |
| Douglas Fir | 25 m | 67 years | 40.57 | -105.09 |

Adding geographic information allows us to ask additional questions, such as:

> **Are older trees concentrated in particular areas?**

This ability to connect data to location is one of the fundamental strengths of geospatial analysis.

---

## Major Areas of Geospatial Science

Geospatial science encompasses several related disciplines and technologies.

### Geographic Information Systems (GIS)

**Geographic Information Systems (GIS)** are computer systems used to store, manage, analyze, and visualize geographic data.

GIS can be used to combine multiple datasets and examine relationships between them.

For example, a researcher could combine:

- Roads
- Rivers
- Elevation
- Land cover
- Population
- Building locations

to investigate questions about transportation, development, or environmental risk.

---

### Remote Sensing

**Remote sensing** involves collecting information about Earth's surface without directly interacting with the objects being studied.

Common sources of remotely sensed data include:

- Satellites
- Aircraft
- Drones
- Radar
- LiDAR
- Multispectral and hyperspectral sensors

Remote sensing can be used to monitor vegetation, map land cover, measure elevation, identify changes in glaciers, and observe environmental conditions over large areas.

---

### Cartography and Visualization

**Cartography** is the practice of designing and creating maps.

Modern cartography extends beyond traditional paper maps. Interactive maps and web applications allow users to explore geographic information, turn data layers on and off, search locations, and interact with visualizations.

Effective geospatial visualization helps people understand complex spatial information and communicate research findings.

---

### Global Positioning and Location Technologies

**Global Positioning System (GPS)** and other positioning technologies allow us to determine the location of objects and people on Earth's surface.

Location technologies are used in applications such as:

- Navigation
- Field research
- Surveying
- Wildlife tracking
- Transportation
- Agriculture
- Recreation

GPS is one example of a broader category of **Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS)**.

---

### Spatial Analysis

**Spatial analysis** uses geographic data and computational methods to identify patterns and relationships across space.

For example, spatial analysis can help answer questions such as:

- What areas are within a 10-minute drive of a hospital?
- Where are populations concentrated?
- Which locations are most vulnerable to flooding?
- Are disease cases clustered geographically?
- How does temperature vary across a landscape?

Spatial analysis often combines GIS with statistics, programming, and other computational methods.

---

### Geospatial Programming and Data Science

Many modern geospatial workflows involve **programming and data science**.

Programming languages such as Python, R, and JavaScript can be used to:

- Process large geographic datasets
- Automate repetitive GIS tasks
- Analyze spatial patterns
- Create interactive maps
- Access geospatial APIs
- Build web mapping applications
- Work with satellite imagery
- Develop reproducible research workflows

Geospatial programming is particularly useful when working with large datasets or tasks that would be difficult to complete manually.

## What Can Geospatial Science Be Used For?

Geospatial science has applications across a wide range of disciplines.

### Environmental Science

Researchers can use geospatial data to monitor:

- Vegetation
- Forests
- Wildlife habitats
- Water resources
- Wildfires
- Glaciers
- Snowpack
- Climate change

For example, satellite imagery can be used to measure changes in vegetation or snow cover across large areas over many years.

### Urban Planning

Cities can use geospatial information to study:

- Population growth
- Transportation networks
- Housing
- Land use
- Infrastructure
- Public services
- Urban development

Geospatial analysis can help planners understand how proposed developments may affect communities and infrastructure.

### Agriculture

Farmers and agricultural researchers can use geospatial technologies for **precision agriculture**, including:

- Monitoring crop health
- Mapping soil conditions
- Managing irrigation
- Identifying areas of crop stress
- Estimating yields
- Applying resources more efficiently

### Public Health

Geospatial methods can help researchers investigate relationships between health and location.

For example, researchers may examine:

- The geographic distribution of disease
- Access to healthcare
- Environmental exposures
- Food access
- Population characteristics
- Changes in health outcomes over time

### Emergency Management

During natural disasters and other emergencies, geospatial technology can help organizations understand what is happening and where resources are needed.

Applications include:

- Wildfire mapping
- Flood mapping
- Damage assessment
- Evacuation planning
- Search and rescue
- Emergency response coordination

### Transportation

Geospatial technologies are fundamental to modern transportation systems.

Applications include:

- Navigation
- Route optimization
- Traffic analysis
- Public transportation planning
- Infrastructure management
- Delivery logistics

---

## Geospatial Science Is Interdisciplinary

One of the most important things to understand about geospatial science is that it is **not limited to a single discipline or career path**.

Geospatial methods can be combined with fields such as:

- Biology
- Ecology
- Geography
- Geology
- Environmental Science
- Agriculture
- Computer Science
- Engineering
- Public Health
- Business
- Economics
- Sociology
- Political Science
- Archaeology

For example, a biologist might use GIS to study wildlife habitat, while a public health researcher might use the same types of tools to investigate access to healthcare.

The underlying technology may be similar, but the **research questions and data are different**.

---

## Geospatial Technology and Your Career

Learning geospatial skills does not necessarily mean pursuing a career specifically titled "GIS Specialist" or "Geospatial Scientist."

Geospatial skills can complement many different career paths.

You might use geospatial technology to:

- Analyze research data
- Create maps and visualizations
- Work with environmental datasets
- Automate data-processing tasks
- Build interactive websites
- Analyze satellite imagery
- Support business decisions
- Manage infrastructure
- Communicate research findings

For students and researchers, learning geospatial methods can therefore provide an additional set of tools that can be applied to problems in many different disciplines.

---

## Getting Started

There are many ways to begin learning about geospatial science. You do not need to become an expert in every area of the field.

A good starting point is to identify **what you want to accomplish** and then learn the tools that support that goal.

For example:

- **Want to make maps?** Start with GIS and cartography.
- **Interested in satellite imagery?** Explore remote sensing.
- **Want to analyze geographic data?** Learn spatial analysis and statistics.
- **Interested in automation?** Explore Python or R.
- **Want to build interactive maps?** Learn JavaScript and web mapping.
- **Interested in field data collection?** Explore GPS/GNSS and mobile mapping.
- **Working with very large datasets?** Explore geospatial data science and cloud computing.

Geospatial science is a broad and continually evolving field. You can start with the area that is most relevant to your interests and gradually develop additional skills as your projects require them.
